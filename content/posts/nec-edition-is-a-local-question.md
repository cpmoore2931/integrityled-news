The National Electrical Code is not one nationwide switch. NFPA 70 is written nationally. Each state, and sometimes each city, decides which edition is actually in force.

NFPA’s enforcement map, current as of August 3, 2026, shows how wide that split still is. Six states were on the 2026 NEC. Twenty were on the 2023 edition. Fifteen were still on the 2020 edition. Three were on the 2017 edition, and two were on the 2008 edition.

The 2026 book itself is not new to the shelf. NFPA’s Standards Council issued it on August 20, 2025. It took effect on September 9, 2025, which is when governments could start updating their own rules to point at it. Issuing a code and enforcing it are different dates. A job permitted this month can still be inspected under an older edition if that is what the authority having jurisdiction has adopted.

For a commercial lighting package, that difference shows up in ordinary details: where lighting outlets and controls are required, how outdoor equipment is protected, and how emergency and controls wiring is treated when the work is more than a one-for-one lamp change. A fixture swap on an existing circuit is not the same scope as new dimming conductors, new sensors, or a change to an emergency circuit. The edition named on the permit is the one the inspector will use.

Before a schedule is locked, ask two questions in writing. Which jurisdiction issues the permit, and which NEC edition will the inspection use? If the answer is a city amendment on top of a state adoption, get that in the same note. Ordering drivers and emergency gear to the wrong edition is a slow way to lose a week.

This brief restates what NFPA published on its enforcement map. It is not a code interpretation, and it is not a substitute for the adopted text or for the inspector on the job.
